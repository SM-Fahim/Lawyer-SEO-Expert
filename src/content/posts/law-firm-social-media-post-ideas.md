Most lists of social posts for lawyers are built for likes. A like does not sign a client. This list is built for the things a law firm can measure: whether people search your name, whether reviews arrive, and whether your site gets read.

A word on limits first. Social posts are not a magic ranking lever. What they do is feed the signals around your firm: branded searches, review volume, and visits to pages that can rank. Treat them as support for your site, not a substitute.

## What social media can do for search

- **Make your name familiar.** People who see your firm often may search it by name later.
- **Send people to pages that matter.** A post that links to a practice page or guide gives that page real readers.
- **Prompt reviews.** Google says prominence in local results reflects review count and rating ([Google Business Profile help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)). Posts that explain how to leave a review make it easier.
- **Give you material to reuse.** One good answer can become a post, a page section and a Google Business Profile update.

## Ground rules for lawyer posts

- **No promises of results.** Past outcomes do not guarantee future ones. Follow your state's advertising rules on testimonials and case results.
- **No legal advice to strangers.** Explain how a process works, not what a person should do in their case.
- **Respect privacy.** Never post client details or photos without written consent.
- **Do not offer rewards for reviews.** Google lists incentives for reviews as prohibited ([review help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122)).
- **Use the same firm name** everywhere, spelled as on your website.

## Posts that bring in reviews

1. A short thank-you to a client who agreed to be named, with the review link.
2. A plain walkthrough of how to leave a Google review in four taps.
3. A "what a good review tells the next client" post, which asks for detail about the process rather than a star rating.
4. A reply to a recent review, shown as a screenshot with names removed.
5. A reminder at the end of a case milestone, sent as a post or story.

Google allows you to ask customers to use a review link or QR code. Keep the ask low-pressure, and never ask only happy clients.

## Posts that answer what people search

These can be built from your own search data. Look at the questions in Search Console and in the "People also ask" box for your main services.

6. "What happens in the first hour after a car accident" as a checklist.
7. "How long does a typical [case type] take in [state]?" with an honest range and a link to the page.
8. "Three documents to bring to a first consultation."
9. "What a free consultation does and does not include."
10. "Common myths about [your area of practice]," one myth per slide.
11. A one-minute video answering a question a client asked this week.
12. A short explainer on a court term, with a link to your glossary page.

The reason these work is simple. Google's guidance on helpful content asks whether a page gives people a complete, original answer ([creating helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)). A post that answers a real question points to a page that does the same.

## Posts that build local relevance

13. A photo from a local event your firm sponsored or attended.
14. A short note about a courthouse: parking, security, what to expect.
15. A guide to a local process, such as filing in your county.
16. A neighborhood spotlight for a business you use.
17. A post marking a local holiday or community day.
18. A team member's volunteer work in the area.

Local posts help people connect your firm with a place. Use the city and county names naturally, as a person would say them.

## Posts that show the people

People hire people. These posts also support branded searches, because they make your name stick.

19. A "meet the attorney" post: where they grew up, why they chose the practice area.
20. Staff introductions, with a role and one line about what they do for clients.
21. A day-in-the-office photo set.
22. A bar admission, award or speaking engagement. State the facts and nothing more.
23. A short video of the attorney explaining what clients usually get wrong.
24. A welcome post for a new hire.

## Posts that point to your best pages

25. A thread that summarizes a long guide on your site, with the link at the end.
26. A new practice page announcement, with who it is for.
27. A seasonal reminder linked to a relevant page, such as holiday travel and accident advice.
28. A "most read this month" roundup of your site's top pages.
29. A short post updating readers when a law changes and you have updated the page.
30. A before-and-after of an updated guide, with the date.

## Sample post formats

Short outlines you can adapt. Fill in your own facts.

**Question post.** First line: the question a client asked. Next three lines: the plain answer. Last line: the link to the page that covers it in full.

**Checklist post.** A title that names the situation, five to seven numbered steps, and a line that tells people when to call a lawyer. Save the full version on your site and link to it.

**Review request post.** Thank clients who have left reviews without naming anyone. Explain that reviews help the next person understand what to expect. Add the review link and the plain steps.

**Local explainer post.** Pick one place people ask about, such as a courthouse entrance or a records office. Give hours, parking and what to bring. Mark the date, since details change.

**Update post.** When a rule changes, say what changed in two sentences and link to the page you updated. This signals to readers, and to Google, that the page is kept current.

## Writing captions that read well

- Lead with the reader's question or situation, not your firm.
- Use short sentences and plain words. Skip legal terms unless you explain them.
- Include the city or county once, where it fits.
- End with one action: read the guide, ask a question, or leave a review.
- Add a disclaimer line where your bar requires one.

## What to avoid

- Posting only holiday greetings. They fill a calendar and do nothing else.
- Buying followers. They do not hire lawyers and can hurt your reach.
- Sharing every news story about a crash or arrest. It can look like chasing cases, which many bars restrict.
- Leaving comments unanswered. Reply to questions, in general terms, and move case details to a private call.

## Posts for each platform

**Facebook.** Best for local audiences and event posts. Questions, local news and short explainer videos fit. Add the link in the post, not the comments.

**LinkedIn.** Best for business, employment and estate work, and for referrals from other lawyers. Short, practical posts about process do well.

**Instagram.** Photos of the team and office, short explainers on carousels, stories for reminders.

**YouTube.** Longer answers to client questions, with a transcript on a page of your site.

**Google Business Profile posts.** Not social media in the strict sense, but you can reuse the same content for updates and events there.

## Who should write the posts

The lawyer should approve every post, and ideally supply the first draft for questions and answers. Staff can handle photos, scheduling and replies. Agree on a short review rule: nothing goes out that a client could mistake for advice, and nothing names a client without consent. A ten-minute weekly review is enough for most small firms.

Keep a running note of client questions. Every call and intake form is a post idea, and the wording clients use is the wording searchers use.

## A simple weekly rhythm

Aim for a plan you can keep.

- **Week 1:** a question-and-answer post.
- **Week 2:** a people post.
- **Week 3:** a local post.
- **Week 4:** a review or best-page post.

That is four posts a month. Four posts you actually publish beat twenty you plan and skip.

## How to tell if it is working

Look at these, not likes.

- Branded searches for your firm name in Search Console.
- Visits to the pages you linked, in analytics.
- New Google reviews each month.
- Calls and form fills with a source recorded.

If a type of post sends nobody to your site and brings no reviews, stop making it.
