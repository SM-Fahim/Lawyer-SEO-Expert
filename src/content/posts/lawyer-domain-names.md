Your domain is the address clients type, the name in your email and the root of every page you will ever publish. It is also one of the few choices in SEO that is painful to undo. This post separates what a domain does for search from what people only hope it does.

## What a domain can and cannot do for rankings

A domain does three real jobs. It identifies your firm, it carries trust with people, and every link and mention you earn attaches to it. What it does not do is rank you by itself.

According to Google's own [Search Central blog](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2015/07/googles-handling-of-new-top-level), its systems treat new extensions like .com and .org, and keywords in a top-level domain give no advantage or disadvantage in search. The post dates from 2015. Google's documentation says it treats any extension that is not a country code as a generic one ([Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/managing-multi-regional-sites)). A .law site and a .com site start from the same place.

So choose a domain for clarity and permanence. Rankings come from the pages, links and reviews that sit on top of it.

## Extensions compared

| Extension | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| .com | Most firms. People guess it and type it by habit. | Good names are often taken. |
| .law | Firms that want a signal of a verified lawyer. | Restricted, costs more, and some clients mistype .com. |
| .lawyer, .attorney | A fallback when the .com is gone. | Less familiar, easy to confuse in speech. |
| .us | Rarely a first choice. | Looks thin next to .com. |

### About .law

The .law extension is restricted. According to the registry's own announcement of the rules ([Minds + Machines, 2014](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/eligibility-requirements-for-the-new-law-and-abogado-top-level-domains-announced-277868191.html)) and registrars such as [GoDaddy](https://www.godaddy.com/help/about-law-domain-names-32123), you have to be a licensed lawyer or a law firm with a licensed lawyer, and eligibility is checked at sign-up and afterwards. That gives .law a trust cue, because clients and other lawyers know it is not open to anyone.

Check the registry's current rules before you build around it. If you lose eligibility, for example by leaving practice, you may lose the domain.

### About .lawyer and .attorney

These extensions describe the work in the address. They help a person read the link and know what it is. They do not help rankings. Treat them as a second-best choice when the .com is gone, and read the registry's terms for any limits.

### The practical answer

If you can get the .com, take it. If you want .law, also register the .com and redirect it, or the other way round, so mistyped addresses still reach you. Do not run the same content on two domains.

## Should the domain match the firm name

Yes, almost always. When your domain, firm name and Google Business Profile name agree, people find you faster and AI tools and directories connect the mentions to one entity.

Mismatches are common. A firm named "Harlan & Pike Law" at harlanpikelaw.com is clean. The same firm at atty-dallas-injury.net is hard to say aloud, easy to mistype, and forces clients to remember two names.

## Exact-match domains

An exact-match domain is one built from a search phrase, such as dallasinjurylawyer.com. These tempt firms because the phrase looks like a ranking asset.

The problems are practical.

- It reads like an ad, and clients trust a firm name more than a keyword.
- You cannot add a second city or practice without the name becoming wrong.
- The name is hard to protect as a brand and easy for others to copy.
- Those domains are often old, resold and carry past history. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) call out expired domains bought mainly to manipulate rankings.

A domain that includes a keyword naturally, such as "harlanpikeinjurylaw.com", is fine. A domain that is only a keyword is a worse long-term bet.

## Hyphens, numbers and length

- **Hyphens.** Google recommends hyphens to separate words in URLs ([URL structure guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/url-structure)). That is about page paths. In a domain name, hyphens make the address harder to say, and similar sites without hyphens can catch the clients who forget one.
- **Numbers.** "Law4You" or "1stdefense" confuses people on the phone. Is it "one" or "1"? If you cannot say it clearly, skip it.
- **Length.** Short is easier to remember and to fit on a card, a van and a social profile. There is no ranking bonus for it. Twenty characters or fewer is a good working limit.
- **Spelling.** Avoid words with common alternative spellings. Say it to three people and see what they type.

## A short domain checklist

Before you pay, check each point.

1. Does it match the firm name?
2. Can you say it on the phone in one go?
3. Is it free of history? Search the domain, and look it up in the Wayback Machine to see what it used to host.
4. Is the same name free as an email address and on social profiles?
5. Do you own the registration under the firm's name, not a freelancer's?
6. Is auto-renew on, with a card that will not expire?

## Buying an old domain

Premium and previously owned domains cost more and carry risk. Look for spam, adult or pharma content in the archive, and run a backlink check for unnatural links. If the history is bad, the name is not worth the price.

## Moving to a new domain without losing rankings

A move is the riskiest thing you can do to a site. Google's [site move guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) gives the steps. In short:

1. Verify the old and new sites in Search Console.
2. Map every old URL to its matching new URL.
3. Set up permanent 301 redirects, one hop each, to the closest page. Do not send everything to the home page.
4. Update internal links, canonical tags and the sitemap on the new site.
5. File a Change of Address for the old domain in Search Console.
6. Update your Google Business Profile, directories and social profiles.
7. Keep the redirects for at least a year.

Expect rankings to wobble for a while. A move done in an hour with no URL map is the one that causes lasting loss. Plan it for a quiet period and monitor the old and new URLs in Search Console afterward.

## Email on your domain

Use email at your own domain, not a free mailbox. Addresses like name@harlanpikelaw.com look like a real firm, and they keep working if you change providers. Set up the standard authentication records (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) so your messages reach inboxes. Your web host or email provider can walk you through this.

## One domain or one per office

Firms with several offices often ask whether each should have its own domain. For search, one domain with a separate page for each office is usually stronger. Links and reviews then add up on one site instead of splitting across several. Separate domains make sense only when the offices are really separate brands.

If you run a second site for a niche practice, give it its own name, content and links. Do not copy pages between the two.

## HTTPS and the www question

Your site should load only over HTTPS. Choose either the www or the non-www version, and redirect the other to it. Google can treat them as different addresses until you tell it which is preferred, so a missing redirect can split your signals. Check that all four variants (http and https, with and without www) end at one address.

## Subdomains and subfolders

Keep your blog and practice pages in folders on the main domain, such as /blog/ and /practice-areas/. Either can work, but a folder keeps everything under one name and one set of reports, which is simpler to manage.

## Protect the name you choose

Once you pick a domain, register the obvious variants that people might mistype, and point them to the main site with a redirect. Turn on registrar lock, use a strong login with two-step verification, and keep the renewal contact on a monitored firm inbox. A lapsed or hijacked domain takes your email and your rankings with it.

Keep a simple record of where each domain is registered, who the account contact is, and when it renews. Share it with a second person at the firm. This takes five minutes and prevents most domain emergencies.

## Mistakes we see

- Registering the domain under a web designer's account.
- Letting an old domain expire after a rebrand, so someone else picks it up.
- Running a site on two domains with the same pages.
- Changing from http to https, www to non-www, or the domain itself without a redirect plan.
- Choosing a domain that spells a different word when read as one string.
