Criminal defense is an unusual field for search. The person searching is frightened, short of time and often using a phone. Many will call the first firm that answers. This guide explains how those searches work and what a defense site needs. It is meant to be used by a lawyer or a marketing lead, with or without an agency.

I have not run a full SEO program for a defense firm that I can show here, so I am teaching the method and the checks, not quoting results.

## How people search after an arrest

Searches come in stages, and each stage needs a different page.

- **The first hours.** "Lawyer for arrest tonight," "bail hearing near me," "what happens after being booked." The person wants a human, now.
- **The first days.** "How much does a defense lawyer cost," "public defender or private lawyer," "should I talk to police." They compare and look for reassurance.
- **Before and after court.** "What happens at arraignment in [county]," "can charges be dropped," "how to expunge a record." They want to understand the process.
- **Family and friends.** Often a parent or spouse is typing, and they are less likely to know legal terms.

The same case can bring searches from all four. Your pages should answer each stage and lead back to a way to reach you.

## What a defense page should not do

Some habits cost calls on a defense site.

- **Burying the number.** If a person has to scroll to find how to call, many will not.
- **Legal jargon in the first paragraph.** A parent reading at midnight needs plain words first, terms second.
- **Fear as a sales tool.** Dramatic warnings about prison time read as pressure, and they can run into advertising rules.
- **Vague promises.** "Aggressive representation" says nothing a visitor can check. Describe what you do at each stage instead.
- **Thin city pages.** Fifty near-identical pages for fifty towns, with only the name swapped, are the kind of repetition Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) are aimed at.
- **Stale facts.** An old page about bail procedure that is no longer true harms both trust and rankings.

## Pick the work you want

Do not try to rank for every offense in the code. Sort what you handle into three lists.

1. **Cases you want more of.** These get full pages.
2. **Cases you take if they arrive.** These get a short section on a related page.
3. **Cases you refer out.** Do not build pages for these.

Be honest about the second list. A page for a charge you rarely handle can bring calls you then decline, which wastes the visitor's time and yours.

Then pair each wanted charge with the places where you appear in court. A page about felony charges in one county can match a search that a statewide page never will.

## The pages a defense site needs

### Charge pages

One page per charge you want. A good one answers, in this order:

- What the charge means in this state, in plain words.
- What usually happens next, step by step.
- What the person should and should not do now.
- How you can help, and how to reach you.

Avoid copying statute text. Explain the process the way you would to a worried client in your office.

### County and court pages

A page for each court where you appear helps local searches. Include the courthouse address, how to find the clerk, the basics of how that court handles first appearances, and your experience there. Update it when procedures change and show the date.

### Cost and fees page

Cost is one of the most common questions. You do not need to quote a figure for every case. Explain whether you charge flat or hourly fees, what raises cost, what a retainer is, whether payment plans exist, and whether the first meeting is free.

### Process pages

Short pages on bail, arraignment, plea offers, trial and sentencing help families who are searching in the middle of a case. They also give your charge pages something to link to.

### Record clearing

If you handle expungement or sealing, give it its own page. These searches come from people who are not in crisis, so they read more and compare more.

### Attorney pages

Each lawyer should have a page with bar admission, practice focus, and background. Keep claims to what is true and allowed in your state. See the section on advertising rules below.

## Make the site work for a person in a hurry

Google looks at the phone version of your pages when it decides where to list them ([mobile-first indexing](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing)). For a defense site, run these checks:

- A tap-to-call number in the header on every page.
- A short form with name, phone and a free-text box. Nothing else is required.
- No pop-ups that cover the page.
- The text readable without zooming.
- A line that says who answers at night and what a person should do if no one does.

Speed matters too. Google's Core Web Vitals give "good" thresholds of 2.5 seconds for the largest content to load, 200 milliseconds for response to a tap, and a layout shift score of 0.1 or less ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)). Test your key pages on a phone, not just on an office computer.

## Local search for defense firms

Many calls come from the map pack and from the profile itself, without a visit to your site.

- **Name.** Use your real firm name only. Google's [guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say the name should match your real-world name, with no added service descriptions or location details.
- **Categories and services.** Choose categories that match your work, and list the charges you take.
- **Hours.** If you answer after hours, say so. If you do not, do not claim to.
- **Photos.** Real photos of the office and the building help a nervous caller find you.
- **Questions and answers.** Add the common questions yourself and answer them.
- **Multiple offices.** Each office with staff gets its own listing and its own page.

Per [Google's local ranking help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), results weigh match, distance and how well known the firm is, and reviews and links add to the last. You can shape the first and the third, not distance.

## Reviews in a field where clients stay quiet

Defense clients often prefer not to publicize that they needed a lawyer. Expect fewer reviews than in other fields.

- Ask at the end of the matter and offer the review link.
- Tell clients they can write without details, and that a first name or initials is fine.
- Never offer anything in return. Google does not allow offering anything in exchange for a review ([review rules](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114)).
- Reply to each review without confirming the writer was a client or mentioning the case. The reply is public and confidentiality still applies.

Family members and referral sources, such as bail agents or other lawyers, can also review you on the basis of their own experience. Check your state's rules.

## Claims and advertising rules

Defense marketing is closely watched. Before you publish:

- **Specialist or expert.** Many states limit these words to lawyers with a state-recognized certification. Describe your work instead.
- **Results.** Past outcomes do not predict future ones, and many states require wording to say so.
- **Guarantees.** Never state or imply you will win or get a charge dropped.
- **Client details.** Do not share facts that could identify a client without written consent.
- **Testimonials.** Follow your state's rule on whether they need a disclaimer.

Have a lawyer in the firm approve every page. Ask your state bar if a rule is unclear.

## Writing a charge page: a worked outline

Here is how a page on, say, a first-offense theft charge might run. The wording is an example, not text to copy.

1. **Heading and opening.** Name the charge and the county. In two sentences, say what the person is facing and that help is available now, with the phone number.
2. **What happens next.** First appearance, possible release terms, the next hearing. Link to your process pages for each.
3. **What to do and not do.** Do not discuss the case with police or on social media. Keep paperwork. Write down what you remember.
4. **How the case can go.** Describe the usual paths, such as dismissal, diversion, plea or trial, without predicting an outcome.
5. **Who we are.** The lawyers who handle these cases, how long the firm has done so, and which courts it appears in.
6. **Cost and first meeting.** A short note and a link to the fees page.
7. **Questions.** Four or five real ones, answered in plain words.

A page like this is usually between 900 and 1,500 words. Length is not the goal. Complete answers are.

## Links and mentions

Links from other sites tell Google that your firm is known. For a defense firm, realistic sources include:

- Local bar association and criminal defense association directories.
- Legal aid, reentry and community organizations you work with.
- Local news when you comment on a case or a change in law.
- Law school clinics and guest talks.
- Podcasts and publications written for lawyers.
- Chambers and civic groups where you are a member.

Avoid paid link schemes and directories that exist only to sell links. They carry risk and bring no callers.

## Using AI answers

Google says there are no extra requirements to appear in AI Overviews and AI Mode, and that normal search practice applies ([AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features)). So the same pages and facts matter. Once a month, type your main questions into AI tools and note which firms and sources they name. If they cite a directory with wrong details about you, correct it.

## Structured data

Structured data is code that labels the facts on a page, such as your phone and hours. Google documents a local business format for it ([local business data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/local-business)), and the page notes that Google does not guarantee any feature will appear. Treat it as housekeeping that helps machines read your site, not as a ranking trick.

## Tracking what matters

Set these up before you change anything.

- **Call tracking** on the number shown on your site, so you know which pages produce calls.
- **Form tracking** with the page recorded.
- **Google Business Profile** call and direction counts.
- **Search Console** for the searches that show your pages and how often people click.
- **A short intake note** that asks every caller how they found you.

Review the numbers monthly. Look at calls and qualified leads, not traffic alone. A page that gets fewer visits but more calls is doing its job.

## A first-month checklist

1. Search your top three charges in your county on a phone and screenshot the results.
2. Fix your Google Business Profile.
3. Check mobile speed and tap-to-call on your five most important pages.
4. Write or rewrite the cost page.
5. Write the first charge page for the case you most want.
6. Start the review routine.
7. Turn on call and form tracking.
