A personal injury firm pays to get the phone to ring. A CRM decides what happens next. It holds the caller's details, reminds your team to follow up, and records whether the lead became a case. Done well, it also answers the question every owner asks: which of our searches bring in cases?

This guide covers what to ask for, how to compare tools, and what to set up so the data is useful. It does not rank products. Features and prices change, so check each vendor's own pages and get a written quote.

## What a CRM does for a PI firm

A CRM for injury work has two halves that people often buy together.

- **Intake.** Capture the lead, ask the right questions, decide if it fits, and book a consult or send a retainer.
- **Relationship tracking.** Keep a record of every contact with past clients, referral sources and prospects, so no one falls through.

Case management, which tracks deadlines, documents and negotiations once you have a client, is a different product. Some tools combine both. Many firms use a separate intake or CRM tool that hands the case to their case system.

## Features a PI firm should expect

Treat these as a checklist when you talk to vendors.

### Capture

- Web forms that put each entry straight in the system.
- Call logging, ideally with the number dialed, the page the caller saw and the time.
- Text and chat messages in the same record.
- A way to enter leads from referrals, ads and walk-ins by hand.

### Intake script and screening

- Customizable questions: date and place of the incident, injury, treatment, insurance, other lawyers.
- Rules that flag conflicts, statute of limitations dates or cases you do not take.
- A clear status for each lead: new, contacted, consult booked, retained, declined, referred out.

### Follow-up

- Tasks and reminders for each lead.
- Templates for texts and emails that your lawyers have approved.
- Alerts when a new lead has not been contacted within the time you set.
- E-signature for the fee agreement.

### Reporting

- Leads by source, month and practice area.
- Time from first contact to retainer.
- Reasons for declined or lost leads.
- Cost per retained case, if you enter marketing costs.

### Integration

- Connection to your website forms and your phone system.
- Export or sync to your case management system.
- A way to move your data out if you leave.

## Security and confidentiality

Intake records often hold health and incident details from people who never hire you. The ABA's rule on prospective clients says a lawyer must generally keep information from them confidential, even if no engagement follows ([ABA Model Rule 1.18](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_18_duties_of_prospective_client/)). Your state's version may add to this.

Ask every vendor:

- Where is the data stored, and who at the vendor can see it?
- Is two-step login available for every user?
- Can you set different access levels for staff?
- Is data encrypted when stored and when sent?
- Is there an audit log of who viewed or changed a record?
- What happens to your data if you cancel?
- Can you delete a record on request?

Put the answers in writing. If a vendor will not answer, move on.

## The SEO reason to have a CRM

SEO produces traffic, calls and forms. Without records, you only know how many arrived, not which were cases. A CRM linked to your marketing lets you answer three questions.

1. **Which pages bring qualified leads?** A page that earns 400 visits and no cases is a different asset from a page with 40 visits and three retained clients.
2. **Which searches and listings do the work?** Your Google Business Profile, your practice pages and your named searches may each bring a different kind of caller.
3. **Where do leads stall?** If calls arrive and few become consults, the problem is intake, not search.

This matters more as search changes. Google's [AI features guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) asks for no extra steps to appear in AI answers, so ordinary pages and listings still earn the visibility. You need to see which of them bring clients.

## How to set up lead tracking

Most CRMs can do this, but few firms set it up.

1. **Give each channel its own tracking number.** One for the website, one for your Google Business Profile, one for print or referral partners.
2. **Record the landing page.** Add a hidden field on each form that saves the page it was sent from.
3. **Ask the source question.** "How did you find us?" belongs in the intake script as a required field. Callers may say "Google" when they mean Maps, so write down what they typed or saw.
4. **Mark the outcome.** Update the status when a lead retains, declines or goes to another firm.
5. **Tag quality.** Mark a lead as qualified if it fits your case criteria, whether or not it signs.
6. **Review monthly.** Compare the leads from each page and listing with the retained cases.

Google describes local results as based on relevance, distance and prominence ([Business Profile help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)). Your CRM shows which of those three actually bring you qualified leads.

## Questions that separate good tools from bad fits

- Can a new intake person learn it in a day?
- Can you change the intake questions yourself?
- Does it work well on a phone?
- How are duplicates handled when the same person calls twice?
- What does a typical setup involve, and who does it?
- What is the contract term, and the fee to cancel?
- What support do you get, and when?
- Who owns the data, and in what format can you export it?

## How to compare options

Make a short list of three tools. For each one:

1. Write down the price in a quote that names the number of users and what is included. Do not rely on a pricing page if it says "contact us."
2. Run a pilot with real leads for two weeks if the vendor allows it.
3. Have the person who answers the phone, not the managing partner, try the intake screen.
4. Ask a firm of your size that uses it for a reference.
5. Read the terms for auto-renewal and data export.

Score each tool on the same few things: ease of use, reporting, security, integration and cost. Weigh ease of use heavily. A tool that staff avoid produces bad data.

## A simple status list that works

Whatever tool you pick, agree on the lead statuses before you load a single record. A short list keeps reports honest.

1. **New.** Arrived, nobody has spoken to the person.
2. **Contacted.** Someone spoke to them or left a message.
3. **Screened.** Intake questions are done and the lead fits or does not.
4. **Consult booked.** A time is set with a lawyer.
5. **Retained.** The fee agreement is signed.
6. **Declined.** You said no, with a reason recorded.
7. **Referred out.** You sent the person to another firm.
8. **Lost.** They chose someone else or stopped replying.

Each status needs a date. With dates, you can measure how long leads sit in each stage, and spot where your team is slow.

## What to review with your team every month

Bring four numbers to a short meeting: leads received, leads screened as real cases, consults booked and cases retained. For each, note the page or listing it came from. If searches bring many leads but few real cases, the page may be attracting the wrong audience, and the fix is in the writing. If real cases arrive but few retain, look at speed to answer and the first conversation. Reports only help if someone acts on them.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying for features you will not use.** Start with capture, follow-up and reports.
- **Skipping the source field.** Without it you cannot tie cases to marketing.
- **Letting staff skip statuses.** If a lead is never marked retained or declined, the reports are wrong.
- **No owner.** Name one person who keeps the system clean.
- **Sending sensitive details by text or email without thought.** Use the secure channel your CRM offers.
- **Overwriting history.** Keep a log of status changes, not just the latest status.
