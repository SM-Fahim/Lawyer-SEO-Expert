A settlement figure is not what lands in your pocket. The attorney fee, the costs of the case and any medical liens come out first. This calculator shows the math so you can see the take-home amount before you sign anything. It works for injured people checking an offer, and for lawyers who want a simple way to explain a statement to a client.

It gives an estimate, not legal advice. Your fee agreement, your state's rules and the terms of any lien decide the real numbers.

## How a contingency fee works

In a contingency fee arrangement, the lawyer is paid a share of what you recover. If there is no recovery, there is no fee. The share is a percentage written in your fee agreement. Some agreements use one rate. Others use a lower rate if the case settles early and a higher one if a lawsuit is filed or the case goes to trial.

The American Bar Association's model rule says a contingent fee agreement should be in writing, signed by the client, and should state how the fee is figured, including the percentages for settlement, trial or appeal ([ABA Model Rule 1.5](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_5_fees/)). States adopt their own versions, and some set limits on percentages for certain case types. Read your own agreement and ask your lawyer if anything is unclear.

## Fees and costs are different

People often mix these up.

- **The fee** is what the lawyer earns for the work.
- **Case costs** are the out-of-pocket expenses to pursue the claim: filing fees, records, experts, depositions, travel and similar items.

The same model rule says the agreement must also state which expenses come out of the recovery, and whether they are deducted before or after the fee is calculated. That choice changes your take-home amount, which is why the calculator has a switch for it.

### A simple example

Suppose a case settles for $90,000, the fee is one third, and costs are $6,000.

- **Costs taken first:** the fee is one third of $84,000, which is $28,000. You receive $90,000 minus $28,000 minus $6,000, which is $56,000, before liens.
- **Costs taken after the fee:** the fee is one third of $90,000, which is $30,000. You receive $90,000 minus $30,000 minus $6,000, which is $54,000, before liens.

The order cost $2,000 in this example. Your agreement tells you which method applies.

## Liens and other deductions

A lien is a claim on part of your settlement by someone who paid or is owed for your care. Examples include health insurers, government health programs and hospitals. Rules differ by state and by the type of payer. Amounts can sometimes be negotiated, and your lawyer may do this as part of the case. The calculator lets you enter a lien and a reduction percentage so you can see both versions. Treat any reduction as a hope, not a promise.

## What the calculator does not do

- It does not value your claim. It starts from an offer or a settlement amount that you enter.
- It does not know your state's rules on fees, liens or taxes.
- It does not include tax. Some parts of a recovery may be taxable, others not. Ask a tax professional.
- It does not replace the closing statement your lawyer prepares.

## Questions to ask your lawyer

1. What percentage applies at each stage?
2. Are costs taken before or after the fee?
3. What costs do I owe if we lose?
4. Which liens exist, and who is negotiating them?
5. Will you give me a written settlement statement before I sign?
