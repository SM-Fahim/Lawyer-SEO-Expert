## SEO for estate planning lawyers starts with two kinds of searcher

Most planning clients aren't in a hurry. A new child, a marriage or a diagnosis starts the search, and they read about wills, trusts and costs for weeks before they book a meeting.

Probate clients search in the weeks after a death. An executor or an adult child wants the first steps, the court and the fee, and often calls the first firm that explains them plainly.

So a planning site carries two sets of pages with different jobs: patient explainers a planner comes back to, and short, direct pages for a family that needs help this week.

A divorce often sends a client back to update the plan, so that update gets a page of its own. How family firms rank for the divorce itself is on [SEO for family lawyers](/seo-for-family-lawyers/).

[more "See how we study each search first"]

For every search we target, we note who ranks, what sort of site each result is and every question Google suggests beside it. We also save Google's AI answer and the pages it cites, before anyone drafts a line.

Three searches from our research show the range. <q data-no-check>cost of making a will with a lawyer</q> comes from a planner comparing options, and <q data-no-check>how much does a probate lawyer cost</q> from a family that needs help now. <q data-no-check>how to make a living trust without a lawyer</q> comes from someone testing the do-it-yourself route, and a plain page on what a homemade trust can miss gives that reader a reason to return.

We don't drop a topic because one keyword tool shows few searches. We open the real results first, and if Google ranks pages built for that question, the firm gets one as well.

[/more]

## What we build for a wills, trusts and probate practice

Our accounts are personal injury firms, so every example here comes from injury work. The first 90 days run in the order shown under [law firm SEO services](/law-firm-seo-services/). A planning practice adds the five steps below.

[steps]
1. **The firm's lines, before any plan.**
2. **This year's figures, read at the source.**
3. **A page for each document and each kind of help.**
4. **Probate pages for the courts the firm uses.**
5. **A review that only the lawyer can do.**

[more "See how each step works for estate work"]

1. The firm tells us once which work it takes and which it sends elsewhere: planning, probate, trust administration, elder law or estate disputes. One injury firm we write for refers probate out, so its pages mention probate only to say where the firm stops. An estate firm that sends contested estates to another firm gets the same treatment.
2. We collect the law the pages rely on from its source, then check it is current this year, because an official code page can lag behind a new act. Estate figures move: the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/whats-new-estate-and-gift-tax) puts the 2026 basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000, after a law signed in July 2025, so a page still warning about an older figure is wrong today.
3. Wills, living trusts, powers of attorney, health care directives and guardianship for children each draw their own questions, so each gets a page. Every topic has one home page and the rest point to it, so the trust page and the probate page never chase the same search. Two wordings of one search, such as "probate lawyer" and "probate attorney", share a page.
4. Probate runs through a local court, so a probate page earns its place with that court's steps, forms and timing. We write one for each court the firm's offices really use. If a page lists local courts, it lists every one, since a gap reads as a judgment; on one injury account, a court list we inherited had dropped a whole level of appeal.
5. The lawyer marks up each page in a plain sheet, line by line, and every correction becomes a rule the next draft is checked against. Before that, each credential and membership is checked with its issuer. On one injury account, the site claimed a membership the lawyer had never held.

## What question pages did for an injury firm

[slot proof]

Our clearest proof concerns question pages, the kind planning clients read, on a PI firm's site in New Orleans. About three months of groundwork came first. Then three new truck accident pages went up, and within 16 hours every one was in Google's top three; the two that answered questions were at #1, each quoted in Google's AI answers.

A jackknife crash page had gone live a week before those three. At the one-week mark it was still first, and Google's AI was still citing it. That staying power is what a planning firm needs, since its readers come back to the same questions for weeks.

[more "See what Google's AI chose to quote"]

On those pages, the AI quoted the concrete lines: the filing deadline, the advice not to give the insurer a recorded statement, and the named highways. It skipped the general ones. On a planning site, the concrete lines are your state's rule, your county court's steps and what a document costs at your firm.

All the pages it cited were already on page one, which tells us the AI answer follows the ranking. If an AI answer misstates a rule, we go by the statute, and a page that gets the rule right, with its source linked, is the one best placed to take its spot.

[/more]

## Writing for families facing illness or a death

Estate pages deal with dementia, a parent's death and family arguments. Four rules hold on every one we write:

[slot rules]

Nothing on these pages is made up: no invented client stories and no borrowed "experience". Readers are deciding about their health and their money. Our checks have caught medical terms doctors don't use, so words such as capacity and dementia are checked before a page goes live.

[more "See how the contact form treats a family"]

An online questionnaire gathers facts for the lawyer to review. It never produces a finished will on its own, and it never tells a visitor whether their plan is sound.

Speed matters less than on an injury site: a planner who writes tonight is happy with a call tomorrow. The rules still apply. A visitor ticks a box to agree before anyone calls or texts them, and how long the firm keeps their details follows consent law and the bar's rules on prospective clients.

## Referrals, reviews and your firm's name

Much of a planning firm's work comes by referral, from advisers, accountants and past clients. Those clients still search the firm's name before they call, so that search must show a complete Google profile, a page per lawyer and listings with the right address and phone.

Listings drift. On one injury account, the firm had moved an office and the new address appeared nowhere online, its own site included. We fix the website first, then the directories, and edit the Google profile last, so it keeps its reviews.

Referral deals carry rules of their own. Under [ABA Model Rule 7.2](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_2_advertising/), a lawyer may not pay for a recommendation, and a reciprocal referral deal with another professional must be non-exclusive and disclosed to the client. Your state's version decides.

[more "See our review and profile routine"]

Ask for a review when a plan is signed, not while a family is grieving. Google's [review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) bars rewards for reviews and bars asking only the happy clients, so every client gets the same request. It also treats reviews from staff as a conflict of interest.

Testimonials on the site carry a first name and an initial, no more. On one injury account we removed a homepage testimonial about a type of case the firm doesn't handle, and a planning firm's site gets the same check.

The profile lists each kind of work the firm takes as a service, worded like the page it matches, and leaves out anything referred elsewhere. Seminars get event pages with the date, the place and a sign-up form. For referrals, talks and the other ways clients arrive besides search, see [how lawyers get clients](/how-to-get-clients-as-lawyers/).

[/more]

## When will a planning practice see results, and at what cost?

Groundwork comes first, about three months of it, and new pages can't move fast until it's done. From there, the pace depends on how many local firms compete for wills, trusts and probate, and on where your site and profile start.

We count calls and qualified leads, meaning consultations booked by people who want work you take, measured against the same months of last year. For a planning firm we also watch the name search, because that is where referred clients land.

Your monthly figure depends on four things: your market, the kinds of work you want to be found for, your offices and the state of your site today. There's no contract to sit out, and the firm owns what we make.
