## How law firm PPC works, and what you pay

In law firm PPC, the firm pays Google each time someone clicks its ad. Google runs an auction on every search, and its [Ad Rank](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1752122) weighs the bid together with the quality of the ad and of the page it leads to. So a relevant ad on the right page can win a better spot for less.

You pay two separate amounts. The ad spend goes from your firm to Google directly, at a budget you set. Our management fee starts from $500 a month, and Fahim confirms it in writing before anything starts.

The account itself stays in your firm's name, whoever manages it.

[more "See why the account stays in your name"]

A partner or office manager holds the top level of access, and the payments profile is in the firm's name. Our team works through logins of its own, which go when the work ends.

Whoever holds the account holds the history: every search that brought a lead, the page each lead landed on and every conversion Google learned from. That record belongs to the firm, and we work for it.

[/more]

## Count answered calls and qualified leads, not clicks

An ad account learns from whatever it's told to count. If it counts taps on a phone number, or forms nobody checked, Google spends more on the searches that produce them. So we set up the count before the first ad runs.

[steps]
1. **Count the calls that were answered.**
2. **Count every form with one lead event of our own.**
3. **Keep the click with the lead.**
4. **Mark which leads were qualified.**
5. **Send the results back to Google Ads.**
6. **Move the budget to the searches that work.**

[more "See how each counting step works"]

1. A click on a phone link counts taps, and most taps on a desktop never become calls. Call tracking knows which calls were answered and how long they ran, so that is the number we count, and the two are never added together.
2. Google's built-in form tracking misses forms that submit in the background. We set up our own lead event instead, and test it.
3. Google gives every ad click its own ID, which is saved with the lead's details. Every other link we place, such as a profile post's button, carries its own tag, so each visit can be traced to what sent it. Most firms skip this: when we listed every injury firm on the El Paso map, most had no tracking tag on their profile's website link, so they couldn't tell map visits from other search visits.
4. Qualified means the caller's matter is one the firm handles, from somewhere it covers. The intake team decides each one, using a definition the firm agrees once.
5. Google's offline conversion import reports which leads went on to become clients, so the bidding learns from cases rather than clicks. Ask counsel before sending any prospective client's details to an ad platform.
6. Searches that bring qualified leads get more of the budget. The rest get less, or get blocked.

[/more]

Every time the tracking changes, we look at what the browser really sends out, because Google's own tag panel can show a healthy tag that is recording nothing.

## Searches worth paying for, and searches to block

Not every search with "lawyer" in it is worth a click. The examples at the top of this page show the split: someone searching for a car accident lawyer near them may call today, and someone searching for a car accident lawyer's salary never will. <dfn>Negative keywords</dfn> are the words that keep an ad off searches like the second.

Our block list starts from the firm's own answers. Before we plan anything, the firm tells us which cases it takes, which it refers out and which places it serves. The case types it refers out, and the places it doesn't serve, go straight onto the list.

[more "See the searches we block from the start"]

- **Jobs and pay:** jobs, salary, hiring, careers, internship.
- **Becoming a lawyer:** school, degree, course, bar exam.
- **Doing it alone:** template, form, sample, DIY.
- **Free help:** legal aid and pro bono, where the firm doesn't take that work.
- **Looking things up:** court records, case lookup, definition.

We don't block "free" on its own. A search for a free consultation can come from someone ready to call a firm that offers one.

After that, we read the searches people actually typed before each ad showed, and keep adding to the list from them.

[/more]

## Landing pages that take the call

Each ad should land on the page that answers its search, not on the homepage. The page shows the case type and the city in its heading, with a call button and a short form on the first screen.

On a page about one kind of case, the form already knows the case type, so the visitor has one less question to answer.

We hold ads to the same rule as profile posts: nothing points at a page until that page is live and answers the search.

[more "See what happens after the form is sent"]

Visitors tick a consent box before the firm may call or text them back.

The short follow-up questions also sort people from bots. A real person with a case answers them, while a bot fills in the first form and stops. That tells the intake team which calls to make first, and it saves the firm paying its answering service for spam that came in through an ad.

Our [lawyer web design](/lawyer-web-design-development/) work builds these pages to load fast on a phone.

## What can your ads say?

Every search ad counts as lawyer advertising, which puts each headline under the bar's rules. Short ads make that harder. [Google's responsive search ads](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7684791) mix headlines and descriptions into combinations of their own, so a headline can show without the line that gave it context.

So we read the state bar's own advertising rules before we write an ad, and no ad runs without approval from you or your ethics counsel.

[more "See the checks every ad goes through"]

- **Fee lines.** In Louisiana, a "no fee unless you win" line has to say whether the client owes case costs. A fee line goes into an ad only in the exact words the firm has approved for publishing.
- **Results.** No settlement amounts and no case results go in a headline unless counsel approves the exact wording, because any headline can run alone.
- **Names and places.** Where the rules ask for it, an ad names a responsible lawyer, and it only ever names a real office city.
- **Specialist words.** "Specialist" and "certified" stay out unless the lawyer holds that certification.
- **Filing.** Whether an ad must be filed with the bar is the lawyer's call. We flag the question instead of assuming.
- **Rivals' habits.** We don't copy a rival's ad just because it keeps running. A rule broken without a complaint is still broken.

[/more]

## Taking over an account, and how ads sit beside SEO

When we take over an account, its history stays, and the first weeks go on a review rather than changes.

[more "See what the takeover review checks"]

We check who holds access and billing, which actions count as conversions and whether any are counted twice, which searches cost money without bringing a lead, and where the ads show.

An old vendor's tracking left in place can count every lead twice. We move the firm onto accounts it owns outright and retire the old ones without double counting.

[/more]

Ads and SEO do different jobs. Ads can put the firm in front of searchers as soon as Google approves them, and they stop when the budget does. Rankings take longer to earn, and they don't charge by the click. [Google says](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) advertising with it has no effect on a site's organic rankings.

The two do share data. An ad account's best searches show which practice pages are worth building for organic search first, and the cost of that work is on our [law firm SEO pricing](/seo-pricing/) page.
