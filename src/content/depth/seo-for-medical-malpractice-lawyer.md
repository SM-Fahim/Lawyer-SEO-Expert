## Medical malpractice SEO is a screening job first

People looking for a malpractice lawyer are rarely in a rush to call. A patient or a relative reads about the harm, the care behind it, the deadline and whether there is a case at all. Many readers don't have one your firm can take.

So the site does two jobs. It ranks for the harm your lawyers take, and it tells the wrong-fit reader early, on the page, so intake spends its hours on cases you want.

[more "See the searches we leave alone"]

Some searches with "malpractice" in them bring visits and no clients. In our keyword research, <q data-no-check>can you sue a lawyer for malpractice</q> and <q data-no-check>how to become a medical malpractice lawyer</q> are two of them, and we build nothing for them.

The test cuts the other way too. One keyword tool can show almost nothing for a topic that another puts in the thousands, so the search results settle it. When Google gives a topic pages of its own, your site gets one as well.

[/more]

## The pages a malpractice site needs, in the order we build them

Our results come from injury firms, and both send their malpractice inquiries elsewhere. So we know this practice from its edge: on their sites we marked where an injury claim ends and a malpractice claim begins, and offered only the first. How the first 90 days run is on our [law firm SEO services](/law-firm-seo-services/) page; a malpractice plan adds these steps.

[steps]
1. **Which harms you take, and in which states.**
2. **Your state's malpractice rules, read at the source.**
3. **A page for each harm, in a family's words.**
4. **A deadline and pre-suit page for each state.**
5. **Your lawyers' review, kept short.**

[more "See what goes into each malpractice step"]

1. Before planning, your lawyers tell us the harms they take, the states they file in and anything that must stay off the site. The two injury lawyers we write for banned settlement dollar ranges and pain-and-suffering math, and those bans fit a malpractice site too.
2. We collect the rules from the statutes, then check that nothing changed this year. Malpractice adds steps an injury claim doesn't have: in Texas, a claimant must serve an [expert report](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/CP/htm/CP.74.htm#74.351) within 120 days of each defendant's answer, or, if the defendant asks, the court must dismiss the claim. For one injury firm, our checks turned up a deadline that applies only to malpractice wrongful death claims.
3. Each harm gets its own page, named the way families describe it, such as a missed diagnosis. Medical terms are checked against medical sources: one draft on an injury account used "secondary drowning", a phrase doctors don't use, and it came out before review. A page that names local hospitals names every one of them or none.
4. Each state page gives the deadline and the steps before suit, links the statute and shows the date we checked it. When Google's AI answer states a deadline wrongly, a correct page is the opening.
5. Your lawyers see only what a lawyer has to decide: results, case strategy and fee wording. Each correction they make becomes a standing rule, so it never reaches them twice.

[/more]

## A form that screens, and never judges the case

Most malpractice screening still happens on the phone. We move its first part onto the page and the form, with questions like these:

[checklist]
- **When the harm happened, and when it was noticed.** Your lawyers decide which date the deadline runs from.
- **The state where the care took place.**
- **Who gave the care:** a hospital, a clinic, a nursing home or a doctor's office.
- **What harm followed,** as a few plain choices and a short text box.
- **Whether records have been requested,** and whether another lawyer has reviewed the case.

[more "See the intake form we rebuilt"]

One injury firm's old form only sent its answers on the final click, so anyone who gave up partway was lost. Our replacement asks three things up front, then six short questions, and saves each reply as it is tapped.

The firm has the lead's record from the first two fields. People with a real case go on to answer the questions and bots don't, which tells intake whom to call first. A consent box comes before any call or text, and a broken question can never cost the firm the contact details.

[/more]

The form never tells a visitor how strong the case is or what it might be worth. Everyone sees the same closing screen, and then a person calls. On a site where many callers will hear no, that rule matters most.

## What a deadline page did for an El Paso injury firm

[slot proof]

Up top, the search shows a page we wrote for a PI firm in El Paso. Asked about the Texas car accident filing deadline, Google's AI answer lists that page first among its sources.

The same firm's Business Profile logged 9 calls from August 2025 to January 2026, by Google's count. From May to July 2026, it logged 23. A malpractice deadline page is the same kind of page: one state, one rule, the statute linked and the date checked.

[more "See what Google's AI quotes, and gets wrong"]

When Google's AI quoted our injury pages, it took the specific lines: a deadline, a warning about recorded statements to an insurer, the highways where crashes happen. It never quoted a line like "call an experienced attorney today".

It can be wrong, too, so we record what it says for each target search but never use it as a source. Malpractice deadlines, with pre-suit steps and questions about when the harm was found, are easy to state wrongly, which leaves room for the page that states them right.

Every page the AI cited for our clients already ranked on page one. Being quoted starts with ranking.

[/more]

## What does a referring lawyer see when they search your name?

We see malpractice referrals from the sending side: the injury firms we work for pass those inquiries on. The lawyer who refers a case searches your name, and so does the client before calling. Both land on your Business Profile, your lawyers' bios, your verdict pages and your reviews.

Those pages must stand up to a lawyer's reading. We check each badge, membership and bar date with whoever issued it, because labels that claim more than the lawyer holds are a mistake we keep finding on law firm sites.

[more "See how verdicts, reviews and the Map work"]

[The ABA's comment on Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/comment_on_rule_7_1/) warns, in its third note, that even a true verdict misleads if it leads readers to expect that result for themselves. Each verdict therefore carries the context your state requires. Even a lawyer's own memory gets checked: one lawyer we write for left out a career total he couldn't verify.

We ask every client for a review the same way, never only the pleased ones, and replies never mention a patient, a diagnosis or a case.

The Map reaches only so far from an office. A statewide practice keeps one profile per staffed office and reaches the rest of the state through its harm and state pages, with no town pages where it has no office.

[/more]
