## What do law firm SEO services include, part by part?

Our law firm SEO services cover seven parts each month, from practice and location pages to links, reviews and call tracking. One monthly price covers all seven, so nothing the firm needs to rank sits outside it.

Some agencies quote part of this list and bill the rest as extras. The table shows what each part changes for a firm, and where the firm can watch it change.

| Part of the work | What it changes for the firm | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| Practice and location pages | Each case type and office gets a page that can rank | Rankings for those searches, then calls from them |
| Links from relevant sites | Trusted local and legal sites point to the firm | Rankings on harder searches, and visits from those sites |
| Google Business Profile and reviews | The firm shows in the Map pack with fresh reviews | Calls and direction requests in Google's own profile count |
| Website design and content | Visitors who land can see why to call this firm | More calls and forms from the same visits |
| Website maintenance and security | The site stays fast, patched and online | A site that loads every time a client tries it |
| Call and form tracking | Every lead is tied to the page that brought it | A lead count by source, every month |
| Rank tracking and AI answer checks | Progress is measured on the searches that matter | The monthly log of rankings and AI answers |

The parts work as a set. A page with no links behind it struggles in a busy market, and a ranking with no tracking can't show which calls it brought.

Our [law firm SEO pricing](/seo-pricing/) page explains what sets the monthly number.

The seven parts don't all start on day one. In a new account, the first 90 days set the order.

## What happens in the first 90 days of law firm SEO?

The first 90 days of law firm SEO lay the groundwork: tracking, fixes, the Business Profile and the first new pages. For a PI firm in New Orleans, that groundwork took about three months.

Every firm starts from a different place, so read the steps as an example plan, not a timetable for results. Here is that example plan for a new account, step by step.

[steps]
1. **Month 1: count before you change anything.** Set up call and form tracking, and record today's rankings and Business Profile calls. Every later change is measured against this baseline.
2. **Month 1: fix what stops Google reading the site.** Clear the errors that keep pages from being crawled or indexed, and repair broken pages and redirects.
3. **Month 1: bring the Business Profile into line.** Check the name, categories, hours and lawyer profiles against Google's rules, and start the review routine.
4. **Month 2: write the pages for the searches that bring callers.** Build or rewrite the practice, case-type and office pages for the matters the firm wants most. You or your ethics counsel approve each one first.
5. **Months 2 and 3: improve the pages people land on.** Tighten the design and wording where visitors leave, so more of them call or send the form.
6. **Month 3: earn links and check AI answers.** Start outreach to relevant local and legal sites, and record what Google's AI says for the firm's key searches.
7. **End of month 3: read the first numbers.** Compare calls, forms and rankings with the month 1 baseline, then set the order of work for the next quarter.

After that groundwork, the New Orleans firm's page for <q data-no-check>what to do after a truck accident in New Orleans</q> reached #1 within 16 hours of going live. That speed came from the groundwork, so it is no promise for a new account.

Step 3 matters early because the Business Profile decides whether a firm appears in the Map pack at all.

## How does a law firm earn a place in the Map pack?

A law firm earns a place in the Map pack through its Google Business Profile. [Google's guide to local ranking](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says local results rest mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and that no one can pay for a better spot.

The <dfn>Map pack</dfn> is the map and the short list of local businesses that Google shows for a local search. Distance depends on where the office is, so the work goes into the other two factors.

Relevance comes from a complete, accurate profile. Prominence is how well known the firm is, and Google says it draws on reviews and on links from other sites.

Google's profile guidelines treat lawyers as individual practitioners, and the details catch firms out. As of November 2026, [Google's guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) set these rules for law firms.

| Google's rule | What breaks it | What we do |
|---|---|---|
| A lawyer gets a profile if client-facing and reachable there | Profiles for paralegals or other support staff | Profiles only for lawyers clients can reach at that office |
| Two or more lawyers at one office: a firm profile, plus lawyer profiles | Lawyer profiles carrying the firm name or practice words | Name each lawyer profile with the lawyer's name only |
| One lawyer practicing under a firm brand: a single shared profile | Two competing profiles for the same solo office | One profile, named "Firm: Lawyer name" |
| One profile per lawyer, not one per specialty | A DUI profile and a criminal profile for one lawyer | List practice areas as services on one profile |
| The name is the firm's real-world name | Adding "car accident lawyer" or a city to the name | Use the name on the door and the letterhead |
| No profile for a mailing address the firm doesn't work from | A rented address in a city the firm wants | Profiles only where the firm really operates |

### Which review requests break Google's rules?

**Asking every client for an honest review is allowed.** [Google's policy on reviews](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) bans paying or giving discounts for reviews, asking only happy clients, and setting staff quotas for review numbers.

Replies need care as well. [ABA Formal Opinion 496](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-496.pdf), issued on January 13, 2021, says a negative review alone does not let a lawyer reveal client information in a reply. State rules control, so check yours.

A review routine that stays inside Google's policy asks every client the same way and offers nothing in return. [Our post on law firm social media post ideas](/law-firm-social-media-post-ideas/) has posts that ask for reviews.

A complete profile sends people on to the firm's website, and the pages there decide what they find next.

## Which pages does a law firm site need, and when is a city page a doorway?

A law firm site needs a page for each case type it wants and a page for each office it really has. [Google's spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) list pages aimed at specific cities that funnel visitors to one page as doorway abuse.

Plan the site in five layers, each one linking to the layer above it and the layer below.

[slot layers]

A page is thin when it would read the same with another town's name swapped in. When an office serves nearby towns, name them on the office page, where the claim is true, instead of building a page for each.

Pages about legal problems face a higher bar. [Google's guide to helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) gives more weight to trust on topics that could affect a person's health, money or safety. Google calls these Your Money or Your Life topics, so each page should name the lawyer responsible and keep its facts current.

## Links that help a law firm rank, and links that put it at risk

Links help a law firm rank when relevant sites choose to point to it, such as local news, bar groups and legal directories. Google's [link spam rules](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies#link-spam) cover buying links, excessive link swaps and links required by a contract.

We judge a link by whether the site is relevant to the firm and its area, not by a tool's authority score.

Paid placements, such as a sponsorship listing, are fine when the link carries a nofollow or sponsored tag. Google's spam policies say a paid link is no violation once it is marked that way.

## How do Google's AI answers pick a law firm page to cite?

Google's AI answers cite law firm pages that already qualify for normal search results. [Google's guidance on AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) sets no extra requirements for AI Overviews or AI Mode, beyond being indexed and able to appear in Search with a snippet.

As of November 2026, Google says no special schema, AI text file or new markup is needed. So the work stays the same: clear answers on pages Google can crawl, index and quote.

For a PI firm in El Paso, a page on the Texas car accident filing deadline is the first source Google's AI answer cites. The New Orleans page in the hero above is cited three times in Google's AI answer for its search.

One setting can stop a citation outright. Google says nosnippet, data-nosnippet, max-snippet and noindex limit what it shows from a page, so key pages should carry none of them.

A useful AI check records five things, so anyone can run the same check again later.

[checklist]
- The search, typed the way a client would type it.
- The feature: an AI Overview or AI Mode.
- The date the search was run.
- The city it was run from.
- Which firms the answer names, and which pages it cites.

Those five details belong in the monthly log for each key search. No one can promise a citation, and Google itself notes that indexing and serving aren't guaranteed.

A citation, like a ranking, is worth only the calls it brings, and calls are how results get counted.

## How are results counted: calls, qualified leads or rankings?

Law firm SEO results are counted in calls and qualified leads first, with rankings as the early sign. A <dfn>qualified lead</dfn> is a call or form from someone with a matter the firm takes, in a place it serves.

Three counts sit behind the monthly log, and each one answers a different question.

[steps]
1. **Business Profile calls, by Google's own count.** [Google's Business Profile help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9918094) defines a call as each tap on the profile's call button.
2. **Website calls and forms, through our tracking.** Each lead is tied to the page and the source that brought it.
3. **Rankings and AI answers, for the searches behind those leads.** These move first and show whether the work is on track.

Google's figure is one that neither the firm nor we can adjust. That makes it a fair check on our own tracking.

Rankings on their own can mislead. A report can show rising positions while calls stay flat, when the gains come on searches that bring researchers instead of callers.

**Agree on what counts as qualified before the work starts:** the case types, the places and the kinds of matter the firm takes. The monthly count then means the same thing to both sides.

Tracking shows one more thing: whether the calls get answered. A page that ranks but sends evening calls to voicemail loses the lead it earned. Our guide to [PI firm CRM software](/crm-software-for-personal-injury-lawyers/) covers lead tracking from the first call.

## Approvals, ownership and the work we refuse

Four questions usually come up before a firm signs: who approves each page, what changes outside the US, what the firm keeps and which tactics we refuse.

### Who signs off each page under US bar rules?

You or your ethics counsel sign off every page before it goes live, because lawyer advertising rules bind the lawyers, not their SEO vendor. In the US, each state sets its own rules, and most build on the ABA Model Rules.

Fahim reviews every page before it reaches you. These are the points worth checking on each one.

[checklist]
- **Past results.** A true result can still mislead if it suggests other clients will get the same, under [comment 3 to ABA Model Rule 7.1](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_7_1_communication_concerning_a_lawyer_s_services/comment_on_rule_7_1/).
- **Specialist words.** States limit claims like "specialist" and "certified", and some go further than the ABA.
- **Filing.** In Texas, only a firm website's home page may need [filing with the State Bar's Advertising Review Committee](https://www.legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/communications-exempt-from-filing-requirements); the other pages are exempt.
- **Recent changes.** New York's amended advertising rules took effect on June 1, 2026. In an [NYSBA article on the changes](https://nysba.org/understanding-changes-to-new-york-state-lawyer-advertising-rules/), a presenter says firms no longer have to put "attorney advertising" on everything they send.

Our own name uses "expert" for our SEO work only. It is not a legal title or a certification of any kind.

### What changes for firms in England and Wales, Canada or Australia?

Outside the US, the same SEO work answers to different regulators. In England and Wales, the [SRA Code of Conduct](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/standards-regulations/code-conduct-solicitors/) requires any publicity about a practice, including its charges, to be accurate and not misleading.

The same code bars unsolicited approaches to members of the public, except current or former clients. For a solicitor's firm, that puts more weight on being found when people search.

In Canada, each provincial and territorial law society sets its own code. The Federation's Model Code asks for marketing that is demonstrably true, accurate and verifiable. The [CBA's advertising toolkit](https://cba.org/resources/practice-tools/the-ethics-of-advertising-a-toolkit-for-lawyers/the-rules-of-advertising/) notes that Ontario's commentary treats words like "best" and "#1" as breaches.

In Australia, rule 36 of the Australian Solicitors' Conduct Rules bars advertising that is false, misleading or deceptive, as the [Law Society of NSW explains](https://www.lawsociety.com.au/practising-law-in-NSW/ethics-and-compliance/regulatory-compliance/advertising-legal-services). Check which version of those rules applies in your state before a page goes live.

We take on firms outside the US as well, working remotely. Fahim has grown businesses in four countries, New Zealand and Ireland among them.

This is general information about marketing, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and country, so check yours with your bar, law society or ethics counsel.
