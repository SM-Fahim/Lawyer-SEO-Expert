## SEO for DUI attorneys runs on two clocks

A DUI arrest starts two clocks. The court case moves at the court's pace. The driver's license runs on a far shorter one: a window of days, not months, to ask a state agency for a hearing.

Drivers search in that order of urgency: a lawyer nearby on the night of release, the license in the first days, then penalties and court dates. A DUI site needs a page ready for each step before the arrest happens, not after it.

[more "See the searches we plan DUI pages around"]

- **The first hours:** a "near me" search, answered by the Map and the main DUI page.
- **The first days:** what happens to the license, answered by a license page for the state.
- **Before court:** first offense, refusal and penalties, answered by sub-pages.
- **After the case:** interlock and getting the license back.

Each of those questions gets one page that owns it. Two pages chasing one search split it between them, so the plan names an owner for every question before anything is written.

[/more]

## Why the license deadline page comes first

A DUI page earns trust or loses it on the law. In month 1 we collect every rule your pages will state, each read from the statute or the agency's own notice, never from another firm's blog.

One example: in Texas, the [notice of suspension](https://www.dps.texas.gov/internetforms/Forms/DIC-25.pdf) a driver receives allows 15 days to request a hearing. A page that states that rule in its first sentence, links the notice and shows the date it was checked answers the driver's most urgent question.

[slot proof]

Pages like that are what Google's AI quotes. When someone searched <q data-no-check>Texas El Paso car accident filing deadline</q>, Google's AI answer cited our page for a PI firm in El Paso before any other. A license question has the same shape: one number, one rule, one official source.

[more "See how we keep deadline pages right"]

The official code page can run behind a new act, so before a page states a rule, we confirm the legislature hasn't amended it this year. Each license page is checked again whenever the state changes its forms.

We record what Google's AI answer says for every deadline search, but we never use it as a source. When it gets a deadline wrong, the opening is ours: a page that gives the rule correctly, with the notice linked beside it.

[/more]

## The DUI pages we build around it

Our accounts are injury firms, so read this as our method carried over, with what DUI work changes. How we run the first 90 days is on [law firm SEO services](/law-firm-seo-services/). Here is the DUI page set, in the order we build it.

[steps]
1. **A license page for each state you serve.**
2. **One main DUI page, in your state's word.**
3. **Sub-pages only where the rule differs.**
4. **Local pages from public crash records.**
5. **A clear line between DUI and criminal pages.**

[more "See what goes into each DUI page"]

1. It follows the agency's own notice, names the agency the driver deals with and says what to do first. The deadline sits in the opening sentence, with the checked date next to it.
2. Texas calls the offense DWI, and other states use other names. The main page takes your state's word in its title and carries the other names in its text, the way our injury pages keep "car wreck" and "car crash" on one page instead of three.
3. Refusal, under-21, commercial drivers and repeat offenses each earn a page when their deadline, penalty or license result differs from the main page. A big search number alone doesn't earn one: a sub-page that would only repeat the main page stays a section of it.
4. For injury pages, we pulled county DUI crash reports from Texas crash records. The same public data gives a DUI site local context, written for the driver who was charged, never against them.
5. Court pages belong on the criminal side, which every charge heard in that court shares, so DUI pages link to them rather than copy them. That side has its own page: [SEO for criminal defense lawyers](/seo-for-criminal-defense-lawyers/).

[/more]

Spanish pages make sense only if your lawyers or staff really work in Spanish. Then they are written in Spanish, as separate pages, with no translation plugin.

## The profile a driver finds after release

The first search after release usually happens on a phone, near the jail or the court, and the Map answers it. For many drivers, the firm's Google profile is the first thing they see.

Profiles grow slowly from a standing start. One PI firm in New Orleans opened a second profile, which logged about 1 call in July and 9 by October 2026. A DUI profile climbs the same way, so it pays to open it early.

[more "See how we set up a DUI profile"]

The profile carries the firm's real name, with no city or "DUI" added, and only categories that are true. Each service matches a page, such as license hearings or refusal cases, and nothing the firm refers out goes on the list.

The hours shown are the hours someone answers. A late-night promise that rings into voicemail loses the very call it was meant to win.

Reviews trickle in, since few drivers want the arrest known. We ask every client, and never staff or friends: [Google's review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) counts those as a conflict of interest.

Only live visitors ever see the tracking number, swapped in by a script. The site's code and the directories keep the genuine one, so Google's crawler finds a single number for the firm.

[/more]

## What can a DUI page claim?

A won license hearing or a dismissed charge is a past result, and a true result can still mislead. Texas [Rule 7.01](https://www.legalethicstexas.com/resources/rules/texas-disciplinary-rules-of-professional-conduct/communications-concerning-a-lawyers-services), for example, calls a claim misleading when it is substantially likely to create unjustified expectations about results. So each result goes up only with your approval and its context.

These are the shortcuts we won't take on a DUI site:

[checklist]
- **A page for each name of the offense.** The same text under DUI, DWI and OWI reads as doorway pages.
- **A deadline with no source or date.** A wrong number of days does more harm than no page.
- **Hours nobody keeps,** or reviews from staff and friends.
- **"Specialist" without the certification behind it.**
- **A won hearing as a headline.**

[more "See which questions we leave to the lawyer"]

Before we write, we read your state bar's own rules, not someone's summary of them. Some questions belong to the lawyer alone, such as whether a page or a profile post must be filed with the bar, and we flag them instead of guessing.

Questions like that, plus results, fees, strategy and the firm's history, are all the lawyer sees from us. We settle every fact we can check ourselves before a draft goes over, so a review takes minutes.

[/more]
