## SEO for immigration lawyers works in each language you serve

Immigration clients search by case type: a green card for a spouse, asylum, a removal hearing, citizenship. Many search in their first language, and many live far from your office, since a lawyer admitted in one state can represent clients from anywhere before the federal immigration agencies.

So an immigration site needs one page per case type, a real version of each in every language your people speak, and pages that reach clients well beyond the Map.

[more "See how we choose languages and case types"]

At setup, the firm tells us which languages its people really speak, and whether each speaker is a lawyer or staff. We build a language only where someone at the firm can take the call in it, and the site names who that is.

For each case type we record who ranks, every question Google suggests and what its AI answer says. Some searches look big and bring no clients: <q data-no-check>i-9 form</q> names an employer's form, a poor match for a firm that wants consultations.

Clients also use other words than the law does. They type "deportation lawyer" where the court says removal, so both words live on one page. A second page for the second word would only split the firm's strength.

[/more]

## What we build for an immigration practice, in order

Personal injury accounts are the only kind we have, so each example here is an injury one, and the method transfers. The first 90 days are laid out in [law firm SEO services](/law-firm-seo-services/). What immigration work changes is in the five steps below.

[steps]
1. **Case types, languages and red lines, from the firm.**
2. **Federal law and agency pages, read at the source.**
3. **One page per case type, then each language version.**
4. **Office pages for the places the firm really works.**
5. **A dated review on every rule page.**

[more "See how each step works for immigration"]

1. At setup the firm settles, once, the case types it takes and sends away, how its consultation fee is worded, the results we may publish and anything that must never appear. On one injury account, red lines like these arrived after pages were drafted, and some had to come down; since then they come first. Clients with a pending charge need a defense lawyer as well, and the charge searches are covered on [SEO for criminal defense lawyers](/seo-for-criminal-defense-lawyers/).
2. Immigration pages rest on statutes, regulations and agency pages, so we read each one where it is published, and every case we cite is checked for what it held and whether it still stands. AI tools have handed us case citations that don't exist, so nothing reaches a page unchecked.
3. Each case type gets its English page first, then a version written in each other language, never run through a translation tool. Each version has its own address, and the versions name each other in tags Google reads. Under [Google's rules for localized pages](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions), a tag the other page doesn't return is ignored. A site that can't hold language versions may need rebuilding, which our [lawyer web design](/lawyer-web-design-development/) page covers.
4. A firm can take clients nationwide, but the Map follows real places. Local pages are built for the firm's offices, and an office page can name the immigration court and USCIS office its clients attend. A town with no office gets no page.
5. Each rule page has a lawyer who owns it, and its visible "last reviewed" date matches the date in its code. When a rule changes, the page changes that week, and every language version moves with the English one.

[/more]

## Trust on every case page, before the first call

Your clients have been warned, often by USCIS itself, that a notario público may not give immigration legal advice. In many Spanish-speaking countries a notario is a kind of lawyer, so the confusion is real, and careful families check who stands behind a page before they call.

Each case page makes that check easy:

[checklist]
- **The lawyer's name and bar admission,** with a link to the state bar's lookup.
- **Who else the client will talk to,** with paralegals and interpreters named as staff.
- **The first consultation's price, length and languages,** in plain words.
- **No promise of approval or timing,** anywhere on the site.

[more "See the rules on claims and credentials"]

A page's claims answer to a federal rule as well as the state bar. For lawyers who appear before the immigration courts, [8 CFR 1003.102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/1003.102) treats a claim that can't be substantiated as misleading, and allows "specialist" only with certification a state grants or approves.

We check every credential with whoever issued it. On one injury account, a trust badge claimed a higher award tier than the lawyer held, and a bar admission date someone remembered wasn't the one on the official record.

Results get the same care. One lawyer we work with wouldn't publish a career total he couldn't verify, so his site shows a few results he can stand behind, each with a disclaimer.

[/more]

## How fast a new page was found, on an injury site

[slot proof]

Immigration rules can change in a week, so how fast Google finds an updated page matters. We published a page on <q data-no-check>what to do after a truck accident in New Orleans</q> for a PI firm whose groundwork, about three months of it, was already done. Sixteen hours on, Google showed it first, and its AI answer cited the page 3 times.

The speed came from that groundwork, not from the page alone. It is what we build toward for an immigration firm: a site where a page revised the week a rule changes gets picked up fast, though nobody can promise the timing.

[more "See which lines the AI quoted, and why"]

On the truck pages we built, the AI lifted concrete facts, such as the deadline, the advice to refuse a recorded statement and the named roads. Every page it chose already ranked on page one, so we work on ranking first and treat AI answers as following from it.

For an asylum or green card question, the concrete facts are the form, any deadline and the office that handles it, each stated with its date and its source.

[/more]

## The Map, reviews and clients far from your office

The Map shows a firm near its real offices, not across the country. Google allows one profile per location, so clients in other states find the firm through its case pages, and clients nearby find it on the Map as well.

Each office's profile lists a service for every case type the firm takes, named like the matching page, and nothing it refers out.

Reviews need extra care, because a review can reveal a client's status. Every client gets the same request, a first name alone is welcome, and a reply never confirms what the client hired the firm for.

[more "See how we run profiles for two offices"]

Two offices mean two profiles, and each profile's posts are about its own office and street, so Google never sees two copies of one text.

Posts speak to someone picking a lawyer this week. Each names a single case type, gives a reason to choose the firm and sends the reader to that case's page, and a post goes up only once its page is live.

[/more]
