## SEO for employment lawyers starts with the side you represent

Workers and employers search for the same law in different words. A worker types what happened. An HR director types what to do next.

[more "See how both sides share one site"]

Both sides can share one site when each has its own section, lawyer bios, fee page and contact path. A separate brand makes sense only when the lawyers decide the two audiences shouldn't meet on one domain.

As on our injury sites, the site has three parts: pages that win clients, education pages that answer wider questions and point readers to them, and the lawyers' bios. A general question, such as what at-will employment means, belongs to the education part: it brings visits but rarely calls.

## Issue pages that screen before the phone rings

An issue page has two jobs: rank for the worker's problem, and tell them early whether the firm can take it. Every inquiry the firm can't take costs an intake call, so screening is part of the page, not an extra.

[steps]
1. **A page for each claim you want more of.**
2. **A qualifying line near the top.**
3. **A first form question that screens.**
4. **Clear edges with the work you don't take.**
5. **A kind way out for the cases you can't take.**

[more "See how each issue page screens"]

1. Close claims get one page or two by a test: we check the same pair of searches in several large cities, and if the results differ in most, each claim gets its own page. That test added a page to one injury plan that we would otherwise have missed.
2. The line says which cases the firm takes, by issue, by state and, where it matters, by the employer's size.
3. On a page about one claim, the form already knows the claim, so its first question can screen instead: how big the employer is, or when the last incident happened.
4. Our workplace injury pages had to explain workers' comp, a field the firm didn't handle, without offering it. An employment firm's neighboring fields get the same rule: explain them, don't offer them.
5. A short paragraph says where else to go, such as the federal agency's own portal. The page keeps its ranking, and intake stops spending calls on mismatches.

[/more]

## Deadline pages, written from the agency's own rule

A worker asking whether there is still time is close to calling. That makes deadline pages the center of an employment site, and the pages Google's AI is most likely to quote.

In month 1 we collect each deadline your pages will state, from the agency itself. One example: the [EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/time-limits-filing-charge) gives most workers 180 days to file a discrimination charge, or 300 days where a state or local agency enforces a similar law.

[more "See how we keep deadline pages current"]

Each page states the limit in its first sentence, links the agency, shows the date we checked it and gets checked again when the agency changes its guidance. An official page can lag behind a change in the law, so we confirm a rule is current instead of assuming it.

Agency data needs the same care. Building injury pages from state crash records and federal safety data taught us that a dashboard's year can run ahead of its download file, and that two products from one agency can count different things.

One of our injury pages shows where this leads. In El Paso, the car accident deadline page we wrote for a PI firm is the source Google's AI answer cites before any other, and an employment deadline page is built the same way: one rule, plainly stated, with its source beside it.

[/more]

Every result we can show is from an injury firm. One PI firm in New Orleans took 139 calls from its Business Profile between June and October 2026, which Google counts as 39% more than the same months of 2025.

Every firm gets the same yardstick: answered calls and qualified leads, never traffic on its own. For an employment firm, a qualified lead is a claim you take, in a state where you practice, against an employer the law covers.

[more "See how we sort the leads by quality"]

Someone tapping a phone link hasn't called yet. We count only calls the tracking system shows as answered, and taps stay out of the total.

The follow-up questions double as a bot filter. Someone with a real workplace problem answers them, and automated spam never gets past the opening form.

The firm owns that lead data, including the page each inquiry came from. Pages that send many cases the firm can't take get their qualifying line rewritten.

## How do reviews and the Map work when settlements are confidential?

Many settled clients can't discuss their case, and some agreements forbid naming the employer. So we never ask a reviewer to mention the case, and we ask every client the same way, as [Google's review policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) requires, never only the happy ones.

Google lets each lawyer who sees clients at the office hold a profile, but its [guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) rule out an extra profile for a specialty. A separate "wage claims" listing is off the table.

[more "See how we handle results and state pages"]

Confidential settlements leave room for the process, the lawyers' bios and any verdicts on public record. Any result we show is one the lawyer will put their name to, shown with a disclaimer, and each credential is checked with whoever issued it.

Employment rights differ by state, so a page for each state law the firm handles does more than a page for each city. Places where the firm has no office get no town page.

[/more]
